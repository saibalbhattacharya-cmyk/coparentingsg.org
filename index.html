A Message from the Founder

Building a Calmer, Child-Centred Tomorrow

CoParenting SG was founded by Saibal Bhattacharya with a clear, singular vision: to help families in Singapore navigate the emotional and logistical complexities of separation with dignity, structure, and an uncompromising focus on the well-being of the children.

Recognizing that the traditional adversarial process often magnifies tension, Saibal established CoParenting SG as an independent, community-driven resource hub. The platform bridges the gap between legal formalities and day-to-day parenting realities—offering practical tools, Singapore-adapted parenting schedules, and low-conflict communication frameworks.

"Divorce ends a marital relationship, but co-parenting endures for a lifetime. When parents are equipped with structured agreements and mutual clarity, children thrive."

Knowledge & Clarity

Frequently Asked Questions

Factual information on parenting terminology, school schedules, and non-adversarial processes in Singapore.

In Singapore family law, these three legal terms govern distinct aspects of a child's care:

1. Custody: Concerns major, long-term decisions impacting the child’s future—such as education, religion, and significant medical care. Singapore courts commonly award joint custody, encouraging both parents to co-operate on major milestones regardless of marital status.

2. Care and Control: Designates which parent the child lives with day-to-day. The parent with care and control manages daily routines, diet, and ordinary domestic schedules.

3. Access: Outlines the structured time the parent without care and control spends with the child. Access is typically divided into regular weekday/weekend access, public holidays, and school vacation access.

Singapore's primary and secondary schools operate on a four-term Ministry of Education (MOE) calendar. Common co-parenting practices for school holidays include:

• Short Breaks (March & September, 1 week each): Frequently alternated year-by-year (e.g., Parent A has March in odd years; Parent B has March in even years).

• Mid-Year Break (June, ~4 weeks): Typically split into equal two-week blocks or alternating halves.

• Year-End Break (Nov/Dec, ~6 weeks): Divided into two equal three-week blocks, with specific rotations arranged for Christmas and New Year.

Agreements often include a standard protocol requiring 30 to 45 days' advance written notice for overseas holiday travel to ensure smooth passport exchange and itinerary coordination.

Child maintenance agreements in Singapore are designed to cover the reasonable living expenses of the child. Standard categories commonly structured in parenting agreements include:

• Basic Living Costs: Food, daily transportation, pocket money, and clothing.

• Formal Education: School fees, textbooks, uniforms, and mandatory school excursions.

• Tuition & Enrichment: Agreed supplemental academic tuition, music, sports, or language programmes.

• Medical & Healthcare: Medical consultations, dental care, hospitalisation insurance premiums, and optical expenses.

Parents often agree to maintain a joint monthly accounting record and share ad-hoc major expenses equally or proportionally based on relative income.

Therapeutic Justice is an overarching framework adopted by Singapore's Family Justice Courts (FJC). Rather than treating divorce as an adversarial contest with "winners and losers," the court views it as a family in transition needing practical, emotional, and relational support.

The court promotes non-adversarial dispute resolution, counselling, and mediation to assist parents in focusing on sustainable solutions for their children. The goal is to reduce hostility and foster an ongoing collaborative parental relationship.

No contested court hearing is required if both parents reach an amicable consensus. Parents in Singapore can formulate an agreed Parenting Plan through private discussion, accredited community mediation (such as through the Singapore Mediation Centre), or counselling via Divorce Support Specialist Agencies (DSSAs).

Once both parties agree to the terms, the plan can be submitted to the Family Justice Courts as a draft Consent Order through legal counsel, formalising the agreement peacefully and efficiently.
